\chapter{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{ch:conclusion}

\section{Summary of contributions}
This thesis asked whether an agent can learn how to construct its own memory and
whether the learned optimum is task-dependent. The answer, established across
three stages of increasing realism, is yes. We parameterized memory construction
--- storage, abstraction, and retrieval scoring --- by a single vector
$\thetavec$ and optimized it by black-box search, leaving the policy and the
language model untouched. In grid worlds the optimizer recovered visibly
different $\thetavec$ per task and lifted the hardest task from $2.5\%$ to
$27.5\%$ success. On a twelve-system benchmark a ten-dimensional learnable memory
reached the top performance cluster, and a tuned $\thetavec$ generalized within
but not across task families. On six real long-context LLM benchmarks,
corpus-cumulative tuning produced a recency-independent memory whose
end-of-corpus accuracy lift survived held-out testing on two of the three
domain-coherent benchmarks (FinanceBench, CUAD; QASPER did not survive Holm
correction) and held when CUAD was scaled from ten to fifty and on to the
\emph{full} five hundred and ten contracts (the batch lift stays significant while
attenuating with corpus size, $+0.161 \rightarrow +0.144 \rightarrow +0.107$ at
$n{=}6702$; Table~\ref{tab:fullcorpus}), where at the full $281$-paper QASPER
corpus the learned memory is tied for best of seven configurations --- beating
five (its canonical start, the grid-tuned variant, dump-all, stock BM25, and
tuned attention) and tying a fairly-tuned BM25 index ($0.351$ vs.\ $0.367$,
$p{=}0.30$). The
tuning objective is a valid proxy for answer quality: recall@8 and judge score
correlate at pooled Spearman $\rho=0.69$.

The work's distinctive feature is that it audits itself. The headline that a
full-context baseline ``collapses'' was traced to a bug and corrected to an
accuracy \emph{tie}; the contribution of learned, task-adaptive memory at
this scale is therefore reframed as one of \textbf{efficiency and scalability}
--- matching full-context accuracy at roughly $1/18$th the cost --- rather than
raw accuracy. Concretely, on FinanceBench the dump-all and selective answers are
statistically non-inferior (overlapping bootstrap CIs, $0.607$--$0.689$ vs.\ the
tuned cell) while the selective prompt stays flat at $\sim$704 tokens where
dump-all exceeds the 128K context window at $N{\approx}11$ CUAD contracts and
reaches $43\times$ the tokens at the full $N{=}510$. The one-time CMA-ES tuning
cost --- no LLM in the loop, only \recallk{} --- was approximately \$18.5 of
compute per benchmark, amortized over all subsequent queries.

\paragraph{Three results sharpen what ``learnable, task-dependent memory'' buys.}
First, held under a genuinely fair light --- against an Okapi BM25 baseline tuned
on the \emph{same} corpus-recall budget and judged one-by-one at matched document
scope --- the learned memory is competitive but its accuracy edge is
\emph{benchmark-dependent} rather than universal (Table~\ref{tab:fairbaseline}):
it wins on \FB{}, ties on QASPER, and \emph{loses} on CUAD, where relevance is
lexical clause-extraction and a well-tuned sparse retriever is simply the right
tool. The honest reading is that the learned optimum captures semantic and
relational relevance well and offers no free lunch on tasks whose relevance is
surface-lexical; on those, the durable contribution is the efficiency and
scalability argument above, not raw accuracy. Second, the corpus-mode evaluation
is near-deterministic: the replicated \FB{} cell has a cross-seed standard
deviation of $0.012$ (and $0.002$ on the canonical configuration) at
temperature~0, so cross-seed variance is small relative to the effects it
brackets, and the point estimates are not seed artifacts. Third, the
task-dependence of $\thetavec$ is not an obstacle to reuse: a leave-one-benchmark-out
predictor maps cheap, LLM-free corpus statistics to a $\thetavec$ that recovers
$0.80$ of the per-task tuning lift (Section~\ref{sec:thetapredict}),
converting ``re-tune per task'' into a one-shot prediction on all
but the most idiosyncratic corpus (CUAD, $0.37$). Together these establish the
claim in its strongest defensible form: what an agent stores and how it scores
retrieval \emph{can} be learned by a single $\thetavec$, the learned optimum is
genuinely task-dependent, and that dependence is itself largely predictable.

\section{Limitations}
The Stage-1 results are single-seed. Several Stage-3 cells are single-seed
(HotpotQA and LongMemEval were re-run at 100-document scale; the headline
FinanceBench cell is now replicated across three seeds, with a cross-seed std of
$0.012$, confirming corpus-mode evaluation is near-deterministic at
temperature~0 --- so the remaining single-seed gaps are a matter of CI tightening,
not point-estimate risk, and multi-seed replication of the other large benchmarks
remains future work). The CUAD corpus-tuning lift is now measured at the
\emph{full} 510-contract corpus for the learned-memory configurations
($n{=}6702$, Table~\ref{tab:fullcorpus}), and the QASPER cross-method comparison
(against tuned BM25, tuned attention, and dump-all) is at the full $281$-paper
scale; and the CUAD cross-method comparison is now also run over all $510$
contracts for the four principal baselines (tuned BM25, tuned attention, HippoRAG,
Letta), which unexpectedly revealed a scale-dependent \emph{reshuffle} of the
memory-system ranking --- HippoRAG's graph collapses and Letta overtakes it and
\Vt{} (Table~\ref{tab:fullcorpus}). Only the full-context dump-all cell was left
at reduced scale, its $\sim$124K-token-per-question cost at $510$ contracts being
prohibitive while its token-explosion scaling is already established analytically.
Answers are judged by a single (cross-vendor) Claude judge; an independent blind
second pass over a stratified 180-question sample gives quadratic-weighted
Cohen's $\kappa=0.66$ (\emph{substantial}), but because both passes are
Claude-class this bounds judge \emph{self-consistency}, not cross-vendor or human
agreement --- a fully independent second-vendor or human rater remains future
work. Refusal/acknowledgment answers carry validated rule-assisted scores
(population-weighted error $0.028$ on a 300-entry hand-checked sample) rather
than one-by-one judgments. Finally, all Stage-3 results use a single answerer
(\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}) and a single encoder (MiniLM); generalization across
models and encoders is untested.

\section{Future work}
Natural extensions are: (i) multi-seed replication of the larger Stage-3 cells;
(ii) scaling CUAD and the other benchmarks to their full corpora and all
configurations; (iii) a second independent judge with Cohen's $\kappa$; (iv)
revisiting the neural memory controller with a larger optimization budget, now
that the scalar \Vfour{} sets a strong, cheap baseline; (v) larger answerer
models, to test whether the efficiency advantage of learned selective memory
persists when the answerer is stronger; and (vi) making $\thetavec$ itself
adaptive \emph{within} an episode (a per-step controller) rather than fixed per
task, which the present grid results suggest could help on long-horizon
out-of-distribution tasks where a fixed $\thetavec$ fails.

\paragraph{Sharper next steps, grounded in the specific gaps this thesis leaves open.}
Beyond the replication and scaling items above, five extensions target the
limitations that most constrain the present conclusions. (a) \emph{An
independent, non-Claude judge.} Because both judging passes are Claude-class, the
reported $\kappa$ bounds judge \emph{self-consistency}, not agreement with a
different vendor or a human; a blind human or second-vendor rater on the same
stratified sample is the single change that would most strengthen every
accuracy claim, and would recalibrate the fair-baseline verdicts of
Table~\ref{tab:fairbaseline} against a rater with no shared bias with the answerer.
(b) \emph{A second answerer and encoder.} All Stage-3 results fix a single
answerer (\texttt{gpt-4o-mini}) and a single encoder (MiniLM); swapping in a
stronger answerer and an alternative embedding model would test whether the
efficiency advantage of learned selective memory --- and the shape of the learned
$\thetavec$ --- generalize, or merely fit one answerer--encoder pair. (c)
\emph{Multi-hop tasks that actually stress a relational term.} The \wgraph{}
component was \emph{refuted} as a retrieval-load feature on the present
benchmarks and reframed as a storage scaffold; a fair test of a relational graph
term requires tasks whose relevance is genuinely compositional (multi-hop
bridging over stored entities), where a graph-scored retrieval could carry load
that single-passage semantic matching cannot. (d) \emph{A cleaner
accuracy-versus-$N$ probe.} The judged accuracy-versus-$N$ measurement here was
inconclusive; a properly powered mixed-category probe --- controlled for question
difficulty and gold position across scales --- is needed to state whether judged
accuracy is flat under selective memory as $N$ grows, a claim the current data do
\emph{not} support. (e) \emph{Scaling the $\thetavec$-predictor.} The
leave-one-benchmark-out predictor learns from only forty tuned slices across five
benchmarks; extending it to many more corpora, and to richer descriptors, would
test whether corpus statistics predict $\thetavec$ well enough to retire per-task
tuning entirely on all but adversarial domains like CUAD.

\section{Closing}
Memory for LLM agents need not be hand-designed and frozen. A small parameter
vector that governs what to store and how to retrieve can be \emph{learned}, its
optimum is \emph{task-dependent}, and --- measured carefully --- it buys
efficiency and graceful scaling rather than a free accuracy gain. That is a
modest but defensible step toward agents that adapt not just their actions but
the structure of their own memory.
